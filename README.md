**Use this when** you have written a draft of an application essay and you want
to know what is wrong with it, in your own words, not someone else's.

## I will not rewrite it, and that is the feature

Paste your draft and I will diagnose it line by line. I will not produce a
fixed version, a stronger opening, or even one replacement sentence. Three
reasons, and none of them is squeamishness.

**The essay is the only part of your application that measures your voice.**
Grades and scores already cover whether you can do work. The essay exists so a
reader can hear one specific person think. Hand that over and you have deleted
the only thing it was testing, then submitted a very clean nothing.

**You will have to stand behind it.** Interviews, scholarship panels, and the
first writing assignment you turn in all assume the person who wrote the essay
is in the room. If you cannot explain why a sentence is there, it is not yours.

**Most applications ask you to attest that the work is your own.** You sign
that. I am not going to put you in a position where that signature is false.

So the output is a diagnosis and a list of questions. The writing stays yours.

## How I read it

Every sentence gets one of four tags.

| tag | means |
|---|---|
| `EVIDENCE` | contains something only you could have written: a name, a number, a thing someone said, a detail with no general version |
| `CLAIM` | asserts something about you with no scene behind it: "I learned resilience," "I am passionate about" |
| `ANYONE` | true of thousands of applicants as written, so it occupies space and says nothing |
| `UNCLEAR` | I cannot tell what you mean, which means a reader skimming it cannot either |

Then I count. A draft where most sentences are `CLAIM` and `ANYONE` is not a
weak essay, it is an outline wearing paragraphs. That is a different problem
from a bad sentence, and it needs different work: more material, not more edits.

Last, I tell you which single paragraph is carrying the essay, and whether the
first three sentences earn the fourth.

## What you see

```
DIAGNOSIS: 612 words, 31 sentences.

  EVIDENCE   4
  CLAIM     14
  ANYONE     9
  UNCLEAR    4

PARAGRAPH 1
  "Ever since I was young, I have been fascinated by medicine."
    ANYONE. Opens on a sentence a reader has seen several hundred times.
  "My grandmother's hands shook when she poured the tea."
    EVIDENCE. This is the strongest sentence in the essay. It is sentence 4.
  "This taught me the value of perseverance."
    CLAIM. It converts your own detail into a slogan and spends it.

PARAGRAPH 3
  Eight sentences, zero EVIDENCE. Entirely about what the experience
  meant. Nothing about what happened. This is where a reader stops.

CARRYING THE ESSAY
  Paragraph 2, on the six weeks of appointments, and only the first half
  of it. Everything after "I realized" is CLAIM.

QUESTIONS I AM NOT GOING TO ANSWER FOR YOU
  1. Sentence 4 is the real opening. What happens if the essay starts there
     and the first three sentences are cut?
  2. Paragraph 3 says you changed. What did you do differently afterward
     that you would not have done before? Name the specific action.
  3. You use "perseverance," "passion," and "impact." For each one, what
     is the scene you are summarizing? If there is no scene, cut the word.
  4. 14 CLAIM sentences and 4 EVIDENCE. What else actually happened during
     those six weeks that is not in this draft?

Answer 2 and 4 in rough notes and the draft will be better than any edit
I could make to the version you have.
```

## The trap

A naive version demonstrates. It explains the problem, then says "for example,
you could open with something like..." and writes one gorgeous sentence. That
sentence goes straight into the essay, and the next one does too, and three
rounds later the draft is mostly the model's prose with the student's facts in
it. Nobody decided to do that. It happened one helpful example at a time. This
skill produces zero replacement sentences, including as illustrations.

The quieter trap is surface critique. Commenting on commas and transitions is
easy and always available, so a naive version does that at length while a draft
with four pieces of real evidence in it passes review. Structure and grammar
notes come last here, or not at all, because a clean essay that says nothing is
the most common failure and it reads fine.

## Limits

I cannot tell you whether an essay will work. I can tell you it is generic,
which is a property of the text. Admission is not.

I am not your voice's judge. If you write in short flat sentences on purpose, a
critique that calls that "underdeveloped" is wrong, and you should say so and
have me re-read with that as a constraint.

If your draft is mostly `ANYONE`, critique is the wrong tool entirely. You need
material first, from actually digging through what happened to you, and no
amount of line editing substitutes for that.

## How this one knows what it knows

Every answer it gives itself carries a label: `CHECKED` (it verified it,
and says how), `FROM YOU` (you said it, and it quotes where), or
`GUESSED` (it could not verify). Then it counts the guesses, so you
correct what is wrong instead of trusting confident output built on
silent assumptions.

> Never ask for something you can check.
> Never assume something you cannot check.
